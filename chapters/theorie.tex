\chapter{Fundamentals}
\label{ch:fundamentals}
One of the most significant ideas that improved simulation speed by up to two orders of magnitude is decomposing large systems into coupled subsystems that can be integrated independently~\cite{Gomes2017}. To implement the coupling of the field and circuit domains via dynamic iteration, the underlying concepts need to be explained first.

\section{Modified Nodal Analysis}
The equations that govern the behaviour of the circuit subsystem can be written in abstract form as
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:mna}
\begin{align}
    \textbf{M} \frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t} \textbf{x}(t) + \textbf{K} \textbf{x}(t) = \textbf{r}(t) \label{eq:mna-base} \\
    \textbf{v}_C(t) = \textbf{P}^\top \textbf{x}(t) \label{eq:mna-circuit-port-operator}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where, in case of MNA, $\textbf{x}$ denotes the unknown functions of node potentials and the voltage-source and inductor branch currents
\begin{equation*}
    \textbf{x}^\top(t) = \lbrack \textbf{\varphi}^\top(t) \quad \textbf{i}_L^\top(t) \quad \textbf{i}_V^\top(t) \rbrack.
\end{equation*}
And $\textbf{v}_C$ is the voltage at the field-circuit interface and $\textbf{P}$ is the according port-operator.

\section{Dynamic Iteration}
The subsystems are integrated independently for a period of time $T_n \rightarrow T_{n+1}$, where $T_n = [t_n, t_{n+1}]$, using the outputs of neighbouring subsystems as inputs. This is the essence of dynamic iteration~\cite{Gomes2017}. It is a fixed-point iteration technique, i.e., the iteration
\begin{equation*}
	x^{k+1} = \varphi(x^k), \quad k=0,1,2,\dots
\end{equation*}
converges to a fixed point, where $x^* = \varphi(x^*)$.

In the case of a field-circuit coupling, the vector of unknowns $x$ contains the voltage (node potentials) and (branch) current across the interface ports. The fixed point is reached if the voltages and currents of both subsystems converge, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{
        \begin{aligned}
            \textbf{v}_C^{k+1} &= \textbf{v}_C^{k} \\
            \textbf{i}_C^{k+1} &= \textbf{i}_C^{k}
        \end{aligned}
    }_{\text{circuit}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{
        \begin{aligned}
            \textbf{v}_F^{k+1} &= \textbf{v}_F^{k} \\
            \textbf{i}_F^{k+1} &= \textbf{i}_F^{k}
        \end{aligned}
    }_{\text{field}}
\end{equation}
where $V_C$ and $I_C$ are the port voltage and current of the circuit-side, and $V_F$ and $I_F$ are the port voltage and current of the field-side. dd

\section{Coupling Scheme}
Transmission between the subsystems is handled at the ports, i.e., the inputs and outputs of a subsystem. Two independent factors determine the coupling of the field and circuit: the coupling direction and the transmission condition itself.

\paragraph{Coupling Direction} The direction of coupling stipulates which variable from one subsystem (output) is prescribed on another (input) during iteration. For our field/circuit co-simulation, it answers the question: \textit{"Does the field ingest a current or a voltage as its (plain) input?"}. This consideration directly impacts the index of the resulting DAE. It has been shown that voltage-driven field formulations ($\textrm{A}^*$ and $\textrm{T-\Omega}$) result in a lower index system (index-1) and thereby offer better numerical stability than the current-driven alternative (index-2)~\cite{Garcia2020}. Hence, a voltage-driven coupling
\begin{equation*}
    v_F \leftarrow v_C
\end{equation*}
is likely the preferred choice for this work's implementation, see Section~\ref{ch:xyce}, though under some scopes the current-driven approach may be considered, cf. e.g.~\cite{Garcia2017}.

\paragraph{Transmission Conditions} The transmission conditions, also called interface conditions, specify \textit{how} the exchanged variables enter each subsystem as inputs and leave it as outputs. For a voltage-driven coupling, the plain (zero-order) conditions of the field/circuit problem read
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classical-wr}
	v_F \leftarrow v_C \quad \text{and} \quad i_C \leftarrow i_F,
\end{equation}
that is, the field is prescribed the voltage and returns the current. The exchanged quantities are waveforms, $v_C = v_C(t)$ and $i_F = i_F(t)$, which gives the method its name.

Extracting a reduced-order model (ROM) of the field domain improves the coupling, and the better the ROM, the larger the admissible time windows~\cite{Schoeps2010,SchoepsDiss}. Because the spatially discretised magnetoquasistatic field behaves as an inductance-like element~\cite{Garcia2020}, the resulting ROM is itself inductive. Later work established that this inductance is precisely the first-order term of a Neumann-series expansion of the optimal transmission condition~\cite{Garcia2017}.

Following the suggestion of \cite{Garcia2017} to excite the field by voltages rather than currents, the optimal transmission condition follows by an analogous derivation.

Let the field problem be voltage driven within a \textsc{Gauss--Seidel} scheme. The zero-order Dirichlet conditions read
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:tc-gs-plain}
\begin{align}
    v_F^{k+1} &= v_C^{k+1}, \label{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} \\
    i_C^{k+1} &= i_F^{k}.   \label{eq:tc-gs-plain-current}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

The circuit-side condition \eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-current} is generalised to a linear combination of current and voltage,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc-gs-robin}
	i_C^{k+1} - \beta\, v_C^{k+1} = i_F^{k} - \beta\, v_F^{k},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the weighting factor.
It is worth noting, that the field's input condition~\eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} is left plain: unlike the field, an arbitrary circuit does not admit an easily extractable impedance, so its optimisation is cumbersome, cf.~\cite{Garcia2017}.

Eliminating the internal degrees of freedom from the frequency-domain circuit equations yields the port relation $V_C = g_c - \mathbf{x}_P I_C$, with transfer impedance $\mathbf{x}_P(\omega) = \mathbf{P}^\top(\mathbf{A}_c\, j\omega + \mathbf{B}_c)^{-1}\mathbf{P}$ and projected source term $g_c = P^\top(\mathbf{A}_c\, j\omega + \mathbf{B}_c)^{-1} g(\omega)$.
The voltage-driven field returns $I_F = \mathbf{Y} V_F$, where the admittance $\mathbf{Y}(\omega) = \mathbf{Z}^{-1}(\omega)$ is the inverse of the port impedance $\mathbf{Z}(\omega)$ of the MQS problem~\cite{Garcia2017}. Inserting the field response and the plain condition \eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} into \eqref{eq:tc-gs-robin} and eliminating $V_C^{k+1}$ gives the error propagation
\begin{equation*}
    i_C^{k+1} - i_C^{k}
      = \rho(\beta)\,\big(i_C^{k} - i_C^{k-1}\big),
    \qquad
    \rho(\beta) = \big(\mathbf{I} + \beta\,\mathbf{x}_P\big)^{-1}
                  \big(\beta - \mathbf{Y}\big)\,\mathbf{x}_P .
\end{equation*}

In order for the fixed point iteration to converge, the error across iterations has to contract, i.e., $\lvert\rho(\beta)\rvert < 1$. Since $\mathbf{x}_P$ is invertible, optimal convergence $\rho(\beta) = 0$ is attained for
\begin{equation*}
    \beta = \mathbf{Y}(\omega),
\end{equation*}
so that the optimised circuit-side transmission condition reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc-optimal-voltage-driven}
    i_C^{k+1} - \mathbf{Y}(\omega)\, v_C^{k+1}
      = i_F^{k} - \mathbf{Y}(\omega)\, v_F^{k}.
\end{equation}

Since $\mathbf{Z} \approx j\omega\mathbf{L}$ is the low-frequency approximation of the exact port impedance (leading term of the Neumann series, corresponding to the magnetostatic inductance), the admittance inherits $\mathbf{Y} \approx (j\omega\mathbf{L})^{-1}$ in the same regime.
As in the current-driven case, this frequency-domain expression must be approximated in our time-domain implementation, where $j\omega$ corresponds to a differentiation in time~\cite{Garcia2017}.
The resulting condition,
\begin{equation*}
    i_F(t) \approx \textbf{L}^{-1} \int v_F(t) \, \textrm{d}t
\end{equation*}
represents the dual of the flux-linkage condition of the current-driven scheme.
Since the underlying inductance is relatively cheap to extract (a single magnetostatic solve), it could be re-evaluated at successive working points throughout the transient to track the magnetic saturation effect of the field subsystem, at the cost of repeated extraction; this is not implemented in this work. Eddy-current effects are not covered in this first-order approximation of the inductance -- those dynamics are compensated by the lagged correction term $\Delta i_F^{k} := i_F^{k} - \mathbf{Y}(\omega)\, v_F^{k}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:tc-optimal-voltage-driven}

\paragraph{Note on Algorithms} Though we used a \textsc{Gauss--Seidel} scheme (Einzelschrittverfahren) to obtain a solution of the DAEs in this implementation, other algorithms such as \textsc{Gauss--Jacobi} (Gesamtschrittverfahren) are common, too. While the former offers faster convergence, the latter has better parallelizability~\cite{Bartel2013}.
