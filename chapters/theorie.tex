\chapter{Fundamentals} \label{ch:fundamentals}
Decomposing large systems into coupled subsystems that can be integrated independently is a key strategy that can improve simulation speed by up to two orders of magnitude~\cite{Gomes2017}. This approach, known as \textit{domain decomposition}, follows the principle of \textit{divide et impera} (Latin for \textquote{divide and conquer}). Before the coupling between the field and circuit domains can be realised through dynamic iteration, the underlying concepts must first be introduced.

\section{Modified Nodal Analysis}
The circuit subsystem can be modelled as a directed graph with an incidence matrix $\mathbf{A}$ that dictates the layout of the lumped circuit elements. \textsc{Kirchhoff}'s circuit laws form the foundation of lumped-element network analysis. In matrix form, they are written as
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{A} \mathbf{i}(t) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{u}(t) = \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{\varphi}(t),
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf{i}$ is the vector of branch currents and $\mathbf{\varphi}$ is the vector of node potentials.
Based on these rules, a system of DAEs that governs the behaviour of the circuit subsystem can be written in abstract form\footnote{Throughout this document, we retain generic vector/matrix notation even where the two-terminal (single port) scenarios reduce the port quantities to scalars. This is done in anticipation of multiport generalisation.} as

\begin{equation*}
    \gls{Mmna} \frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t} \mathbf{x}(t) + \gls{Kmna} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{r}(t) \label{eq:mna-base}
\end{equation*}
where, in the case of MNA, $\mathbf{x}$ denotes the unknown functions of node potentials and the voltage-source and inductor branch currents
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{x}^\top(t) = \lbrack \mathbf{\varphi}^\top(t) \quad \mathbf{i}_L^\top(t) \quad \mathbf{i}_V^\top(t) \rbrack.
\end{equation*}
Moreover, we introduce
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{vC}(t) = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{x}(t) \label{eq:mna-circuit-port-operator} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{P}\gls{iC}(t),
\end{equation*}
where \gls{vC} is the voltage and \gls{iC} the current at the field-circuit interface, and $\mathbf{P}$ is the corresponding port operator.
Using this notation, the (black box) field subsystem may be abstractly written as
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{x} = f(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}).
\end{equation*}

\section{Dynamic Iteration} \label{sec:dynamic-iteration}
The subsystems are integrated independently for a period of time $\mathcal{T}_n \rightarrow \mathcal{T}_{n+1}$, where $\mathcal{T}_n = [\gls{tn}, T_{n+1}]$ with window size $\gls{Hn} := T_{n+1} - \gls{tn}$, using the outputs of neighbouring subsystems as inputs; see Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic-iteration}. This is the essence of dynamic iteration~\cite{Gomes2017}. It is a fixed-point iteration technique, i.e., given a function $\phi: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, the iteration
\begin{equation*}
	\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}), \quad k=0,1,2,\dots
\end{equation*}
converges to a fixed point, where $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^*)$, \cite{Dahmen2008}.

In the case of a field-circuit coupling, the vector of unknowns $\mathbf{x}$ contains the voltage (node potentials) and (branch) current across the interface ports. The fixed point is reached if the voltages and currents of both subsystems converge, i.e.,
\begin{equation*}
    \underbrace{
        \begin{aligned}
            \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k)} \\
            \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k)}
        \end{aligned}
    }_{\text{circuit}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{
        \begin{aligned}
            \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} \\
            \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}
        \end{aligned}
    }_{\text{field}}
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}$ and $\mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}$ are the port voltage and current of the circuit-side, and $\mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}$ and $\mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}$ are the port voltage and current of the field-side.

\section{Coupling Scheme} \label{sec:coupling-scheme}
Transmission between the subsystems is handled at the ports, i.e., the inputs and outputs of a subsystem. Two independent factors determine field-circuit coupling: the coupling direction and the coupling conditions themselves.

\subsection{Coupling Direction} The direction of coupling stipulates which variable from one subsystem (output) is prescribed on another (input) during iteration. For our field-circuit co-simulation, it answers the question: \textit{"Does the field ingest a current or a voltage as its (plain) input?"} This consideration directly impacts the index of the resulting DAE.

Roughly speaking, the index of a DAE corresponds to the highest derivative of an input function that enters the subproblem \cite{SchoepsDiss}.

\begin{example}[DAE index]
    Take the current-driven case, where the circuit's port-current $\gls{iF}(t) = \gls{iC}(t)$ is prescribed as the input function on the field subsystem, thereby \textit{driving} it: The variable to solve for is the field's port-voltage \gls{vF}, which, in a predominantly inductive problem is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{vF}(t) \approx \gls{Lport} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \gls{iF}(t).
\end{equation*}
The input function requires one differentiation here. If we look at the voltage-driven case on the other hand, the field solves for
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{iF}(t) \approx \gls{iF}_{,0} + \gls{Lport}^{-1} \int_{0}^{t} \gls{vF}(\tau) \mathrm{d}\tau,
\end{equation*}
requiring no differentiation, but an integration instead, which actually has a smoothing property.
Based on this simplistic example, one might easily understand that a lower-index system is desirable, since numerical differentiation amplifies noise and discretisation error.
\end{example}

The example above ignores the algebraic constraints of the full coupled DAE. In the case of more general systems, it has been shown that voltage-driven field formulations result in a lower-index system (index-1) and thereby offer better numerical stability than the current-driven alternative (index-2)~\cite{Garcia2020}. Hence, a voltage-driven coupling
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{vF} \leftarrow \gls{vC}
\end{equation*}
is likely the preferred choice for this work's implementation; see Chapter~\ref{ch:xyce}, though under some scopes the current-driven approach may be considered, cf. e.g.~\cite{Garcia2017}.

\subsection{Coupling Conditions} The coupling conditions, also called transmission conditions in the context of \textsc{Schwarz} coupling, specify \textit{how} the exchanged variables enter each subsystem as inputs and leave it as outputs, see e.g. \cite{Gander2004}. For a voltage-driven coupling, the plain (zero-order) conditions of the field-circuit problem read
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classical-wr}
	\gls{vF} \leftarrow \gls{vC} \quad \text{and} \quad \gls{iC} \leftarrow \gls{iF},
\end{equation}
that is, the field is prescribed the voltage and returns the current. The exchanged quantities are waveforms, $\gls{vC} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}(t)$ and $\gls{iF} = \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}(t)$, which gives the method its name.

Extracting a reduced-order model (ROM) of the field domain improves the coupling, and the better the ROM, the larger the admissible time windows~\cite{Schoeps2010,SchoepsDiss}. Because the spatially discretised magnetoquasistatic field behaves as an inductance-like element, the resulting ROM is itself inductive~\cite{Garcia2020}. Later work established that this inductance is precisely the first-order term of a Neumann-series expansion of the optimal transmission condition~\cite{Garcia2017}.

Following the suggestion of \cite{Garcia2017} to excite the field by voltages rather than currents, the optimal transmission condition follows by an analogous derivation.

Let the field problem be voltage-driven within a \textsc{Gauss--Seidel} scheme. The zero-order Dirichlet conditions read
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:tc-gs-plain}
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}, \label{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} \\
    \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}.   \label{eq:tc-gs-plain-current}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

The circuit's input condition \eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-current} is generalised to a linear combination of current and voltage,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc-gs-robin}
	\mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} - \gls{beta} \, \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} - \gls{beta} \, \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)},
\end{equation}
where \gls{beta} is a weighting factor.
Only the circuit's input condition is generalised. The field's condition~\eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} is left plain. The field admits an easily extractable impedance, but an arbitrary circuit does not, so optimising the field-side condition analogously is cumbersome \cite{Garcia2017}.

Eliminating the internal degrees of freedom from the frequency-domain circuit equations yields the port relation $\gls{vC} = \mathbf{r}_{\text{C}} - \mathbf{x}_{\text{P}} \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}$, with transfer impedance $\mathbf{x}_{\text{P}}(\gls{omega}) = \mathbf{P}^\top(\gls{Mmna}\, j\gls{omega} + \gls{Kmna})^{-1}\mathbf{P}$ and projected source term $\mathbf{r}_{\text{C}} = \mathbf{P}^\top(\gls{Mmna}\, j\gls{omega} + \gls{Kmna})^{-1} \mathbf{r}(\gls{omega})$.
The voltage-driven field returns $\gls{iF} = \gls{Yport}\,\gls{vF}$, where the admittance $\gls{Yport}(\gls{omega}) = \gls{Zport}^{-1}(\gls{omega})$ is the inverse of the port impedance $\gls{Zport}(\gls{omega})$ of the MQS problem~\cite{Garcia2017}. Inserting the field response and the plain condition \eqref{eq:tc-gs-plain-voltage} into \eqref{eq:tc-gs-robin} and eliminating $\mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}$ gives the error propagation
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} - \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k)}
      = \gls{rho}(\gls{beta})\,\big(\mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k)} - \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k-1)}\big),
    \qquad
    \gls{rho}(\gls{beta}) = \big(\mathbf{I} + \gls{beta}\,\mathbf{x}_P\big)^{-1}
                  \big(\gls{beta} - \gls{Yport}\big)\,\mathbf{x}_P ,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix.

In order for the fixed point iteration to converge, the error across iterations has to contract, i.e., $\lvert\gls{rho}(\gls{beta})\rvert < 1$. Assuming $\mathbf{x}_P$ is invertible, optimal convergence $\gls{rho}(\gls{beta}) = 0$ is attained for
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{beta} = \gls{Yport}(\gls{omega}),
\end{equation*}
so that the optimised circuit-side transmission condition reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc-optimal-voltage-driven}
    \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} - \gls{Yport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}
      = \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} - \gls{Yport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)},
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to the transmission condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc-optimal-current-driven}
    \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} = \gls{Zport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} - \gls{Zport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}
\end{equation}
derived from the current-driven setting in \cite{Garcia2017}. This leads to the following result.
\begin{corollary}
    The optimisation of the circuit-side transmission condition is impartial to the input function of the field subsystem, i.e., imposing \gls{vC} results in the same optimal transmission condition~\eqref{eq:tc-optimal-current-driven} as imposing \gls{iC}.
\end{corollary}
They can be viewed as Thévenin and Norton equivalents, cf. \cite{Nshimiyimana2016}.
This is expected, as both port variables appear on each side of either optimised transmission condition, tied together by the exact port operator (\gls{Zport} or \gls{Yport}). That operator reproduces the field's response exactly and, trivially, would guarantee convergence of the scheme in one iteration ($\gls{rho} = 0$).

Since $\gls{Zport} \approx j\gls{omega}\gls{Lport}$ is the low-frequency ($\gls{omega} \tau \ll 1$) approximation of the exact port impedance (the leading term of the Neumann series, corresponding to the magnetostatic inductance; see \cite{Garcia2017}), the admittance inherits $\gls{Yport} \approx (j\gls{omega}\gls{Lport})^{-1}$ in the same regime.
As in the current-driven case, we must approximate this frequency-domain expression in our time-domain implementation, where $j\gls{omega}$ corresponds to a differentiation in time~\cite{Garcia2017}.
The resulting condition
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{iF}(t) \approx \gls{Lport}^{-1} \int \gls{vF}(t) \, \textrm{d}t
\end{equation*} % TODO should it be called differential inductance/admittance like in Garcia2017?
represents the dual of the flux-linkage condition
\begin{equation*}
    \gls{vF}(t) \approx \gls{Lport} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \gls{iF}(t)
\end{equation*}
of the current-driven scheme, where \gls{Lport} is constant.
Since the underlying inductance is relatively cheap to extract (a single magnetostatic solve), it could be re-evaluated at successive working points throughout the transient to track the magnetic saturation effect of the field subsystem, at the cost of repeated extraction; this is not implemented in this work. Eddy-current effects are not covered in this first-order approximation of the inductance; those dynamics are compensated by the lagged correction term $\Delta \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} := \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} - \gls{Yport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:tc-optimal-voltage-driven} or $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} := \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} - \gls{Zport}(\gls{omega})\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:tc-optimal-current-driven}, respectively.

The impedance of our ROM is complemented by a resistance \gls{Rport}, which may also be extracted by a port measurement of the subsystem\footnote{The resistance can be calculated according to \textsc{Ohm}'s law, e.g., by imposing a DC current of \qty{1}{\ampere} at the terminals and measuring the resulting potential difference.}. Hence, expanding the low-frequency approximation of transmission condition~\eqref{eq:tc-optimal-current-driven} to
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} + \bigg( \gls{Rport} + \gls{Lport} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \bigg)\, \big( \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} - \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)} \big)
\end{equation*}
in the time domain, see Figure~\ref{fig:coupling-gs-rom}.

\paragraph{Algorithm} With this information, an algorithm in the time domain can be written as follows.
% --- System 1: circuit, given input Delta v_{\text{F}}^{(k)} ---
\begin{align} \label{eq:algorithm-1}
  \gls{Mmna} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
    + \gls{Kmna}\, \mathbf{x}^{(k+1)} + \mathbf{P}\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{r}(t) \\
  \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)} &= \mathbf{P}^{\top} \mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
\end{align}
with the coupling condition
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}(t) &= (\gls{Rport} + \gls{Lport} \, \mathrm{d}_t)\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}(t)
    + \Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}(t) \label{eq:tc-optim-time} \\
    \Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}(t) &:= \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}(t)
        - (\gls{Rport} + \gls{Lport} \, \mathrm{d}_t)\, \mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}(t) \label{eq:tc-optim-time-corr}
\end{align}


\begin{description}
  \item[Step 0.] Set $k = 0$ and $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(0)}(t) = \Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F},0}(t)$,
    and extract \gls{Lport}.
  \item[Step 1.] Solve the circuit system with condition \eqref{eq:tc-optim-time}
    for the given input $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k)}(t)$ to obtain the output
    $\mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}(t)$.
  \item[Step 2.] Solve the field system for the given input
     $\mathbf{v}_{\text{C}}^{(k+1)}(t)$ to obtain $\mathbf{i}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)}(t)$, and update the correction
    $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)}(t)$ according to \eqref{eq:tc-optim-time-corr}.
  \item[Step 3.] If not converged, set $k = k+1$ and go to Step~1.       
    Otherwise proceed with
    $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(0)}(t) = \Delta \mathbf{v}_{\text{F}}^{(k+1)}(t_{n+1})$
    for $t \in T_{n+1}$, set $n = n+1$ and go to Step~1.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Note on Solving the DAEs} Though we used a \textsc{Gauss--Seidel} scheme (Einzelschrittverfahren) to obtain a solution of the DAEs in this implementation, other algorithms such as \textsc{Gauss--Jacobi} (Gesamtschrittverfahren) are common, too. While the former offers faster convergence, the latter is more parallelisable~\cite{Bartel2013}.
